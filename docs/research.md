# Group once, then explain chapters in parallel

The best-supported way to turn a large PR into reviewable chapters is a **hybrid**: cheap deterministic structure (hunks, identifiers, test-to-source links) fed as evidence to an LLM that groups by *intent*, plus a targeted critique pass. On shared benchmarks, plain LLM grouping already beats every pre-LLM method, and structure plus review add roughly 6 to 12 more points. pr-guide's current design (deterministic hunks, one Opus call, exactly-once enforcement) already sits in the "zero-shot LLM" tier, which beats graph and GNN methods. Its gaps are no structural evidence, no review or repair step, and a *global layer ordering* ("foundations, then users, then UI, then tests") that the review-ordering research ranks below keeping related changes together. On speed, decode and thinking tokens dominate wall-clock time, not input size. The three biggest levers are dropping effort from `high` to Opus 5.5's own default `medium`, shrinking the single call into a small grouping "skeleton", and writing chapter explanations in parallel, a pattern with a published 1.9 to 2.4x speedup. The evidence is uneven. Grouping-beats-structure and size-hurts-review are well supported. Chapter-level PR decomposition, chapter ordering, flow diagrams and Claude-specific latency have no direct evidence, so pr-guide needs a small benchmark of its own (grouping agreement against human labels plus wall-clock p50/p90) before it can call any configuration optimal.

## LLM intent grouping beats 15 years of dependency graphs, but real PRs stay hard

Research on "untangling" composite changes has gone through four generations, and the ranking on shared benchmarks is consistent. Barnett et al.'s ClusterChanges (ICSE 2015) linked diff regions by def-use relations and found that **over 40% of 1,000 Microsoft Office changesets could be decomposed**; 16 of 20 developers agreed with its partitions ([Morning Paper](https://blog.acolyer.org/2015/06/26/helping-developers-help-themselves-automatic-decomposition-of-code-review-changes/)). Flexeme added identifier "name flows" to a multi-version dependence graph ([UCL](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10107163/2/Barr_Flexeme-%20Untangling%20Commits%20Using%20Lexical%20Flows_AAM.pdf)). SmartCommit built a hunk graph with refactoring and textual-similarity edges and ran for 9 months with 83 engineers ([FSE 2021](https://2021.esec-fse.org/details/fse-2021-papers/20/SmartCommit-A-Graph-Based-Interactive-Assistant-for-Activity-Oriented-Commits)). UTango and HD-GNN learned the clustering with graph neural networks ([NJIT](https://digitalcommons.njit.edu/fac_pubs/2517)). When ColaUntangle (2025) re-ran all of these on the same C# and Java datasets, the order was stark:

| Method | C# Acc^c | Java Acc^c |
|---|---|---|
| Barnett def-use (2015) | 7 | 17 |
| Flexeme (2020) | 34 | — |
| SmartCommit (2021) | — | 30 |
| UTango (2022) | 46 | 34 |
| HD-GNN (2024) | 50 | 38 |
| **LLM zero-shot, single prompt** | **60** | **59** |
| LLM zero-shot + CoT | 61 | 63 |
| ColaUntangle (multi-agent) | 72 | 69 |

Source: ([ColaUntangle](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16395v3)). Acc^c is the share of changed statements placed in the correct group.

A single zero-shot LLM prompt, which is what pr-guide does, **beats the best GNN by 10 to 21 points**. Atomizer (ICSE 2026) confirms the direction with its own re-run (57.0 vs HD-GNN 50.5 on C#), and its margin grows to **+16.2 points on the largest commits** (over 7K graph nodes), the regime pr-guide targets ([Atomizer](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01233)). Treat the absolute numbers with care. The two papers disagree: ColaUntangle's plain zero-shot LLM (60) beats Atomizer's full system (57) on nominally the same benchmark, and they put SmartCommit at 30 vs about 42. Setups differ, so only within-paper comparisons are trustworthy.

The ablations show *which* additions pay. In ColaUntangle, removing the multi-agent consultation (critique) step cost the most (71 → 59), removing implicit semantic context cost 7%, and removing explicit dependency context cost only about 3%. A 1-hop neighbourhood of context was the best cost/benefit point ([ColaUntangle](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16395v3)). In Atomizer, removing Tree-sitter slicing context cost 8.9 points, removing "what → how → why" intent reasoning cost 7.2, and removing the review loop cost 5.8 ([Atomizer](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01233)). The pattern is consistent: **intent reasoning and a review step matter most, and structural context helps but is secondary**. Def-use alone, the classic signal, is the weakest method on every table.

The sobering result is AtomicCommitBench (2026), the only benchmark built from *real* work: 800 squashed same-author sessions from 10 Python projects, median 12 hunks across 6 files ([AtomicCommitBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.03332)). The best agent (GPT-5.4) reached **ARI 0.459 against the developer's own commit split, versus 0.340 for a trivial one-group-per-file baseline**. Two of four LLM agents scored *below* that file baseline. Real sessions scored 0.333 ARI lower than synthetic tangles. Adding a dependency analyzer plus hunk-role profiler gave weak models significant gains (+0.075 and +0.049 ARI) and strong models none (+0.005, −0.006). The named failure modes map directly onto chapter grouping: **"same-file lumping"** (unrelated hunks grouped because they share a file) and **"support-hunk drift"** (tests and helpers attached to the nearest textual change instead of the change they support).

Three things follow for pr-guide. First, its core bet, LLM grouping over hunks, is the right one. Every alternative with numbers is worse. Second, with Opus as the grouper, deterministic dependency signals will add little (strongly suggested by AtomicCommitBench's strong-model result). They become valuable the moment pr-guide moves grouping to a cheaper, faster model, which is exactly what the speed recommendations below do. Third, a critique pass is the best-evidenced quality addition. It is also the most expensive: consultation took 34.9 s of ColaUntangle's 71.6 s per commit. But 70 to 85% of cases converge in one round ([ColaUntangle](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16395v3); [Atomizer](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01233)), which argues for running review only when cheap heuristics flag a suspicious grouping. pr-guide already feeds the PR title, description and commit headlines to the model, which matches the evidence: adding the commit message raised tangle-detection F1 from about 0.73 to 0.88 ([Opu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08263)) and cut Hamming loss by up to 31% ([Koh et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21298)). It sends headlines only, so full commit bodies are a cheap addition.

One caution applies throughout. Every benchmark measures *atomic-commit* recovery, not 3 to 8 *reading chapters*. Coarser groups are probably easier, because there are fewer boundary decisions, but nobody has measured that, and no benchmark of chapter-level PR decomposition exists.

## Related-first ordering beats layer-first ordering, and the last chapter gets the least attention

pr-guide's ordering rule, "foundations and config first, then code that uses them, then UI, then tests and docs", is the same layer order CodeRabbit shipped in June 2026: schema → logic → API call sites → UI → tests ([CodeRabbit](https://coderabbit.ai/blog/coderabbit-review-reads-a-pr-how-author-would-explain-it)). Industry has converged on it. The research says something subtler. Baum, Schneider and Bacchelli's ordering theory has six principles, and the first three set the priority: **group related change parts as closely as possible; provide information before it is needed; and when the two conflict, grouping wins** ([EMSE 2019](https://sback.it/publications/emse2019a.pdf)). The theory is a partial order. It says nothing about which of two *unrelated* groups comes first. "Foundations first" is a reasonable way to apply principle 2, but a strict global layering breaks principle 1 whenever a type change and its only caller, or a function and its test, land in different chapters.

pr-guide's GROUPING.md already gets most of this right. It says to group by purpose, that one chapter usually spans "the code, its config, its tests", and to order hunks inside a chapter definition-before-callers. The weak point is the chapter-ordering line, which pushes tests and docs to the end, and the model may read it as a reason to peel tests off into a trailing chapter. That matters because position bias is the most robust ordering finding. In Fregnan et al.'s experiment (n=106), reviewers had **64% lower odds of finding one defect type when its file came last** rather than first, and mining 219,476 PRs showed earlier files get more comments ([Fregnan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.04259)). A 2026 mining study of 330,343 PRs found latent bug rates rising from 56.7% at file position 1 to 61.5% at position 30, with each extra file cutting comment odds by about 8.7% ([Rahman et al., preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22610)). Tests-first review does not reduce production-defect detection and finds more test defects ([Spadini et al.](https://serg.ewi.tudelft.nl/publications/test-driven-code-review-an-empirical-study/)). Among reviewers who leave alphabetical order, 29% of PRs touching both tests and code are reviewed test-first ([Bouraffa et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10654)). A trailing "tests" chapter is therefore the worst slot for tests.

Ordering's measured effect is real but small. Baum's controlled experiment (n=50, 46 professionals) found the theory-preferred order **+44% faster in median defects per hour, but not significant** (p=0.26). The file-preserving comparison was larger (d=0.43), and the effect was bigger for low-working-memory reviewers (+91%, p=0.054). The study was underpowered by its own analysis ([EMSE 2019](https://sback.it/publications/emse2019a.pdf)). The same experiment produced a large, significant **size** effect: a matched defect was found 76% of the time in a 31-LOC change versus about 36% in a 233 to 490 LOC change. Size thresholds rest on weaker evidence. SmartBear/Cisco's 2,500-review study saw defect density fall sharply above about 200 LOC, though the vendor write-ups disagree on 100–300 vs 200–400 ([Cohen](https://agileconnection.com/article/largest-case-study-code-reviews-ever)). Google's median change is 24 lines ([Sadowski et al.](https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf)). pr-guide's "3 to 8 chapters" has no empirical basis. Chapter *size* should drive the count, not a fixed range.

The evidence-aligned ordering for pr-guide is concern-first: each chapter is one feature or intent, carrying its own types, config and tests, read bottom-up inside (definition before use). Chapters are then ordered by cross-chapter dependency, and **ties are broken by risk or importance, not by layer**, so the riskiest code gets the early-position attention. Test-only or docs-only chapters stay legitimate when they truly stand alone. Reviewers' strategies diverge more as PRs grow ([Bouraffa et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10654)), and 66% of 1,355 surveyed developers want customizable order ([Rahman et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04207)), so the HTML should keep chapters freely navigable.

On payoff, pr-guide should set expectations honestly. The one controlled decomposition experiment (n=28) found **fewer wrongly reported issues and more context-seeking, but no increase in defects found** and no gain in understanding rationale ([di Biase et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10978)). The proven benefit is less noise and more confidence, not more bugs caught.

## Explanations should lead with "why", stay grounded, and avoid implying safety

Rationale is reviewers' top information need, and they get it mainly from the change description ([Tao et al., FSE 2012](https://www.cse.ust.hk/%7Ehunkim/papers/tao-fse2012.pdf)). Reviewers also need completeness, consistency and risk, and how modified code is used ([Pascarella et al.](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/information-needs-in-contemporary-code-review)). GROUPING.md's "what changed, why, and what a reviewer should check" fits this well. Two risks deserve guardrails. Hallucination is common in code-to-text generation: **about 20% of generated commit messages and about 50% of generated review comments** contain hallucinations ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08661v1)). And LLM output anchors attention. Developers adopted 89% of LLM-flagged issues and concentrated on the highlighted locations, "potentially overlooking other areas" ([arXiv 2411.11401](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11401v3)). Meta's MetaMateCR trial *raised* review time by over 5% when reviewers felt obliged to verify AI output ([Meta](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.13499)). pr-guide's exactly-once check protects structure, not prose. A cheap deterministic grounding check closes part of that gap: every backticked identifier in an explanation should appear in that chapter's hunks or the PR text. Explanations should also avoid wording that implies unmentioned hunks are safe. Copilot-for-PRs data shows the generated text developers delete or rearrange most, and the intent and testing information they add, which supports short, intent-first prose ([Xiao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08967v1)). No study shows that flow diagrams or overview flowcharts improve review outcomes. pr-guide's diagram is a reasonable bet with no evidence for or against it, and it is a candidate to drop if it costs meaningful latency.

## Decode and thinking, not input size, set pr-guide's wall-clock

Wall-clock time is roughly time-to-first-token (prefill plus up-front thinking) plus (thinking + visible output tokens) ÷ output speed. OpenAI's latency guide puts the asymmetry bluntly: halving output tokens can roughly halve latency, while halving the prompt saves only 1 to 5% ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization)). Artificial Analysis measures **Opus 5.5 at about 76 output tok/s, with a 37.6 s time-to-first-answer-token at high effort**, which for a reasoning model includes thinking ([AA](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/claude-opus-5-5-high)). Opus 5.5's adaptive thinking cannot be disabled. Its depth is controlled by `effort`, which applies to all output tokens, and **Opus 5.5 defaults to `medium`**. Anthropic tells developers to run an effort sweep rather than carry settings over, and its earlier guidance warns that the highest effort levels can overthink structured-output tasks ([Effort docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/effort)). pr-guide pins `high`.

A rough budget for pr-guide's current call is an estimate, not a measurement. 150k input tokens probably cost on the order of 10 to 20 s of prefill, extrapolated from Anthropic's 2024 figure of 11.5 s uncached for 100k tokens ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/prompt-caching)). High-effort thinking plausibly adds tens of seconds. Then 3 to 6k tokens of visible JSON at about 75 tok/s adds 40 to 80 s. The total is likely **1.5 to 2.5 minutes, mostly spent generating tokens**. The schema makes this worse in two ways. Chapters come *last*, after summary, steps and the flow diagram, so nothing reviewable streams early. And each chapter's prose is generated serially inside one decode stream.

The structural fix is Skeleton-of-Thought: one call produces a compact skeleton, and independent sections are expanded in parallel. Across 12 models this gave **1.89x to 2.39x speedups (Claude 1.83x)** ([Ning et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15337)). SoT does badly when later sections depend on earlier ones. Chapter explanations largely don't, *given* the skeleton, which fixes titles, hunk assignments and the PR summary. For pr-guide, the skeleton call keeps the whole-PR grouping judgment on one model with full context, and it emits only summary, chapter titles, ordered hunk ids, a one-line gist each, and the flow, perhaps 500 to 1,500 tokens instead of 3 to 6k. Then 3 to 8 parallel calls each see only their chapter's hunks plus the skeleton and write 150 to 400 tokens. Total time becomes skeleton + slowest chapter. One API detail shapes the design. Changing `output_config.format` or top-level effort invalidates the prompt cache, and parallel requests only hit a cache after the first response has begun ([Caching docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). The chapter calls should therefore carry *small, chapter-specific* inputs, not re-send the full diff hoping for cache hits.

The remaining levers rank below that. **Model mix**: Sonnet 5.5 decodes at about 129 tok/s, about 1.7x Opus ([AA](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/claude-sonnet-5-5)), and chapter prose is the low-judgment part, so it can move down a tier first. Grouping should move only if the benchmark says so, and then with deterministic signals added, since those are what rescue weaker models. **Fast mode** gives up to 2.5x output tok/s on Opus 5.5 with the same weights, at 2x price, but it is a waitlisted research preview on the Claude API only ([Fast mode docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/fast-mode)). That makes it an opt-in Action setting, not a default. **Prompt caching** helps only re-runs on an unchanged prefix. Put instructions first, keep effort, schema and speed constant, and keep the schema byte-stable, with no per-run hunk-id enums, so the compiled grammar (cached 24 h) is reused ([Structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). **Progressive rendering**, by reordering the schema or simply by fan-out, cuts perceived latency even where total time is unchanged. **Input trimming** matters mostly for quality, not speed. Long-context degradation is documented ("lost in the middle"; LongCodeBench drops of 29% → 3% for Claude 3.5 Sonnet at 1M tokens) but only for older models ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172); [LongCodeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07897)). For very large PRs, PR-Agent's tested pattern of ranking files, dropping deletion-only hunks, listing overflow files by name, and falling back to at most 4 parallel chunk calls is a sensible ceiling strategy ([PR-Agent](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/qodo-ai/pr-agent/main/docs/docs/core-abilities/compression_strategy.md)). No vendor publishes walkthrough latency. The one figure found, CodeRabbit at "7 to 30+ minutes", is third-party and unverified ([aicodereview.cc](https://aicodereview.cc/blog/coderabbit-vs-claude-code)). A sub-minute guide would still be competitive.

## The recommended architecture and what to change first

The evidence points to a five-stage pipeline. **Stage 0 (deterministic, about 0 s)** parses hunks as today and computes evidence: identifiers defined and used per hunk (regex first, Tree-sitter later), test↔source pairing by path and name, whitespace-only, comment-only and rename-only flags, a per-hunk LOC count, and the existing generated-file flag. **Stage 1 (skeleton call, Opus 5.5 at `medium`)** reads the PR text plus a compact evidence index and returns summary, steps, flow and, for each chapter, title, ordered hunk ids and a one-line gist. **Stage 2 (deterministic validation and targeted repair)** runs the exactly-once check. Instead of silently dumping misses into "Remaining changes", it sends one short repair call listing only the unplaced ids, and it flags chapters that look suspect: oversized (over about 400 changed LOC), singletons, single-file lumps despite cross-file evidence, or tests separated from their pair. **Stage 3 (conditional critique)** re-asks the model only about flagged chapters. **Stage 4 (parallel fan-out)** writes chapter explanations concurrently, on Sonnet 5.5 if the benchmark holds, each streaming into the HTML as it completes, then runs the identifier-grounding check. In the Claude Code skill, the same split applies: Claude in-session produces the skeleton, and the explanations can come from parallel subagents or a call to the script. No evidence exists on in-session latency, so measure it there too.

### How strong each claim is

Strongly evidenced: LLM grouping beats structural and graph methods. Critique and intent reasoning are the largest accuracy contributors. Larger review units reduce defect detection. Later positions get less attention. Output tokens, not input, dominate latency. Parallel section generation roughly halves time. Inference, reasonable but untested for this task: that pr-guide's chapter-level grouping behaves like atomic-commit untangling; that concern-first ordering beats layer-first for chapters; that SoT quality holds for code explanations (SoT itself flags coding as a weak category); that `medium` effort preserves grouping quality; and the prefill and thinking-time estimates above. No evidence at all: chapter-level PR decomposition accuracy, the right chapter count, bottom-up vs top-down order inside a group, the value of flow diagrams, Claude-specific untangling accuracy, and current-model long-context quality at 150k tokens.

### The benchmark that turns this into "optimal"

Closing those gaps needs a small, cheap benchmark of 30 to 50 PRs. Mix sizes (median and large), languages and kinds (feature, refactor, mixed, dependency bump). Have two people independently partition each into chapters. Their mutual ARI is the ceiling, because human labels are noisy: line-level annotators disagreed on about 11% of lines in Herbold et al.'s dataset ([Herbold et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06244)). Score each configuration on **grouping agreement** (ARI against each annotator, compared with the one-chapter-per-file and per-directory baselines, which a configuration must clearly beat); **orphan rate** (the share of hunks in "Remaining changes"); **ordering violations** (chapters that use an identifier first defined in a later chapter, computable from the Stage 0 index); **test co-location** (the share of test hunks in the same chapter as their source pair); **grounding** (the share of backticked identifiers found in the chapter's hunks); and **speed** (wall-clock p50 and p90, time to first chapter rendered, thinking vs text output tokens from `usage`, and cost). Run the grid {Opus high, Opus medium, Opus low, Sonnet medium} × {single call, skeleton + fan-out} × {evidence index on, off}. Adopt the fastest configuration whose ARI drop stays inside the inter-annotator gap. AtomicCommitBench's Python episodes ([AtomicCommitBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.03332)) provide a free external check on the grouping step alone. A follow-up with 10 to 20 reviewers rating coherence and usefulness, as in ColaUntangle's 4-person study, would cover what ARI cannot.

## Conclusion

The research reframes pr-guide's problem. Grouping is no longer the hard algorithmic part: a single strong-model prompt already beats a decade of specialised graph tooling, and the remaining accuracy comes from cheap structure, targeted self-review and repair, not from bigger prompts. The real opportunity is architectural. pr-guide currently pays for global context *and* for long serial prose in one high-effort decode stream. Splitting it so the expensive judgment happens once, compactly, and the prose happens in parallel probably makes the tool both faster and easier to steer, because each stage then has its own measurable failure mode.

The second shift is in what pr-guide should promise. The evidence supports "fewer false alarms, less cognitive scatter, faster orientation", not "more bugs found". The ordering that best serves that is the reverse of the industry layer cake: keep each idea's code, contracts and tests together, and spend the reviewer's freshest attention on the riskiest chapter. Whether that intuition holds for chapter-level guides is something only pr-guide's own benchmark can show, and running it would produce the first public evidence on the question.
